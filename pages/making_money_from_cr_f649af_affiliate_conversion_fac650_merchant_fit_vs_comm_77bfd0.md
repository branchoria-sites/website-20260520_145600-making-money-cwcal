## Introduction

A high affiliate commission does not automatically produce high affiliate income. In many cases, the merchant with the lower commission rate generates more revenue per visitor because more users actually complete the purchase, pass approval checks, or return fewer orders. For affiliate websites, the important metric is not headline commission percentage but effective earnings after conversion friction, stock availability, checkout quality, delivery expectations, and approval rates are taken into account.

This matters because affiliate optimisation happens after the click as well as before it. Two [merchants]({{ 'untitled/merchants/' | relative_url }}) may sell the same product at similar prices, yet produce dramatically different earnings because one has poor mobile checkout, long delivery times, aggressive account creation prompts, or frequent out-of-stock products. Ecommerce usability research consistently shows checkout abandonment remains extremely high, with Baymard reporting average cart abandonment around 70%. ## Why commission rate is not the whole offer

Affiliate dashboards often encourage publishers to focus on visible commission numbers: 12% versus 5%, £40 per lead versus £15, or recurring versus one-off payments. But those numbers are only meaningful if the merchant converts traffic efficiently and approves transactions consistently.

A lower-paying merchant can outperform a higher-paying competitor when:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Checkout completion rates are higher
* Mobile usability is better <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/real-cost-poor-mobile-usability-e-commerce-why-its-costing-fsgxc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: linkedin.com">[linkedin.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">linkedin.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">LinkedInThe Real Cost of Poor Mobile Usability in E-Commerce...According to a recent report from Baymard Institute, the average mobile c...</span></span></span>
* Products stay in stock
* Delivery times are shorter
* Pricing is more trusted
* Refund rates are lower <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://primer.io/blog/understanding-average-cart-abandonment-rates-industry-benchmarks-and-best-practic" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: primer.io">[primer.io]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">primer.io</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Understanding Average Cart Abandonment Rates | BlogConversion rate, average order value, and revenue per visitor may appear lower than th...</span></span></span>

* Lead approval rates are higher

</div>

This changes how affiliate sites should compare programmes. The useful question is not “Which merchant pays the most?” but “Which merchant produces the highest approved earnings per visitor from this traffic source?”

A simple comparison shows the difference:

If 1,000 visitors send 100 clicks to each merchant:

* Merchant A produces roughly 0.7 approved sales
* Merchant B produces roughly 3.8 approved sales

Even with the lower commission percentage, Merchant B can produce more total revenue because the conversion path leaks less.

## Merchant checkout friction changes affiliate economics

Merchant-side friction is one of the biggest hidden variables in [affiliate revenue]({{ 'untitled/revenue-leaks/' | relative_url }}). An affiliate page can perform well, generate highly qualified clicks, and still produce disappointing earnings if the merchant creates unnecessary obstacles after the user leaves the affiliate site.

Baymard’s long-running ecommerce research repeatedly finds major checkout usability weaknesses across ecommerce sites, with average cart abandonment remaining around 70%. ### Checkout design can erase commission advantages

Consider two electronics retailers:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--benefit" markdown="1">

* Retailer A pays 8% commission
* Retailer B pays 3% commission

</div>

Retailer A appears superior on paper. But if Retailer A forces account creation, has poor mobile usability, and regularly reveals shipping costs late in checkout, many affiliate-driven visitors may abandon before payment.

Meanwhile Retailer B may offer:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* Guest checkout
* Faster page loads
* Trusted payment options
* Clear delivery dates
* Simpler mobile forms
* Better stock visibility

</div>

Baymard specifically notes that difficult guest checkout visibility and checkout complexity contribute directly to abandonment. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://baymard.com/lists/cart-abandonment-rate" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: baymard.com">[Baymard Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">baymard.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">cart abandonment rate</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Baymard Institute50 Cart Abandonment Rate Statistics 2026 – Cart &amp; Checkout22 Sept 2025 — A comprehensive list of 50 different ecommerce...</span></span></span>

### Mobile traffic amplifies merchant differences

This effect becomes stronger on mobile-heavy affiliate sites.

Many affiliate publishers focus heavily on content optimisation while overlooking whether merchants actually convert mobile visitors effectively. Yet mobile ecommerce usability problems remain widespread, with Baymard documenting thousands of mobile usability issues during testing. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/real-cost-poor-mobile-usability-e-commerce-why-its-costing-fsgxc" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: linkedin.com">[LinkedIn]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">linkedin.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">LinkedInThe Real Cost of Poor Mobile Usability in E-Commerce...According to a recent report from Baymard Institute, the average mobile c...</span></span></span>

A merchant with weak mobile checkout can destroy conversion rates for:

* Product [review pages]({{ 'untitled/review-pages/' | relative_url }})

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* “Best X for Y” lists
* Voucher and deal pages
* Social traffic landing pages
* Pinterest-driven buying traffic

</div>

For scalable affiliate systems, this means merchant selection should sometimes vary by device profile. A merchant that performs acceptably on desktop traffic may perform poorly on mobile-heavy traffic clusters.

## Stock reliability affects affiliate revenue more than many publishers realise

Out-of-stock products create a hidden conversion tax on affiliate pages.

Many affiliate websites continue sending clicks to unavailable products because:

* Product feeds update slowly
* Manual reviews become outdated
* Evergreen articles are not refreshed
* [Comparison tables]({{ 'untitled/tables/' | relative_url }}) age badly

This creates several revenue problems simultaneously:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Users bounce back to search results
* Merchant trust declines
* Affiliate cookies may never become valuable
* Users restart product research elsewhere
* Competing merchants capture the final purchase

</div>

A lower-paying merchant with stable inventory often outperforms a higher-paying merchant with unreliable stock availability.

This is especially important for:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* Seasonal products
* Consumer electronics
* Fashion and footwear
* Home fitness equipment
* Viral social-media products
* Promotional deal content

</div>

For website-generation systems, stock reliability becomes a repeatable commercial signal. Merchants with consistent availability support scalable evergreen content more effectively because older pages remain commercially functional for longer.

## Delivery expectations can change conversion rates dramatically

Delivery quality is often underestimated in affiliate strategy because affiliates do not directly control fulfilment. But users absolutely include delivery expectations in purchase decisions.

Research into checkout abandonment consistently identifies slow or unclear delivery as a major conversion problem. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.socialintents.com/blog/how-to-reduce-cart-abandonment/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: socialintents.com">[Social Intents]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">socialintents.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">how to reduce cart abandonment</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Social IntentsHow to Reduce Cart Abandonment: 12 Proven Tactics (2026)Baymard found that 21% of shoppers abandoned because delivery was t...</span></span></span>

This matters particularly for:

* UK-focused affiliate sites
* Time-sensitive purchases
* Gift-related traffic
* Emergency replacement products
* Premium-priced items

A merchant offering:

* next-day delivery,
* reliable fulfilment,
* clear delivery dates,
* easy returns,
* and local trust signals

may outperform a higher-paying overseas merchant with:

* customs delays,
* vague shipping estimates,
* higher return friction,
* or uncertain VAT handling.

For affiliate content systems, this affects offer matching logic. UK users searching “best office chair UK delivery” are not merely comparing chairs. They are expressing fulfilment intent. Sending them to a merchant with poor UK delivery performance weakens monetisation even if commissions appear attractive.

* clicks,

### Lead-generation programmes can hide weak economics

High-paying finance, insurance, or SaaS affiliate offers frequently advertise large commissions per lead. But approval filters may be extremely strict.

For example:

* Merchant A pays £80 per lead with a 20% approval rate
* Merchant B pays £25 per lead with a 90% approval rate

Merchant B may generate more stable and scalable income because more user actions survive the approval process.

This becomes particularly important for:

* [comparison pages]({{ 'untitled/comparison-pages/' | relative_url }}),
* lead forms,
* software trials,
* finance affiliates,
* and broadband or utility offers.

A website system optimised around approved EPC rather than headline CPA values will often make better long-term decisions.

### Reversal rates can expose weak merchant fit

High reversal rates frequently indicate:

* poor audience targeting,
* misleading product positioning,
* aggressive upsells,
* low merchant trust,
* or fulfilment problems.

In practical affiliate operations, a merchant with slightly lower payouts but cleaner approvals and fewer refunds is often easier to scale safely across hundreds or thousands of pages.

That reliability matters because scalable affiliate publishing depends on repeatable economics. Predictable conversion behaviour is usually more valuable than occasional high-paying spikes.

## Merchant fit should match page intent

Merchant performance is not universal. The same merchant can perform brilliantly on one page type and poorly on another.

Examples:

Page intentBetter merchant traits“Best budget laptops”Low prices, visible discounts, fast checkout“Premium espresso machines”Strong trust signals, financing, reviews“Same-day flower delivery”Delivery speed and reliability“Cheap web hosting”Simplified signup flow“Business software comparison”High lead approval quality

This means affiliate systems should avoid treating merchants as interchangeable.

A scalable monetisation system benefits from classifying:

* which merchants convert best for informational traffic,
* which merchants suit high-intent buyer traffic,
* which merchants convert mobile users well,
* and which merchants produce reliable approved earnings.

That classification often becomes more valuable than headline commission tables.

## The strongest affiliate pages optimise the full conversion chain

Successful affiliate sites usually evolve away from simplistic “highest commission wins” thinking.

Instead, they optimise:

* [intent matching]({{ 'untitled/intent-matching/' | relative_url }}),
* merchant trust,
* conversion reliability,
* approval quality,
* fulfilment experience,
* and long-term EPC stability.

This changes how scalable affiliate systems should evaluate success.

A merchant is not merely an advertiser attached to a link. It is the final stage of the conversion funnel. If that final stage performs badly, the affiliate page can appear weak even when the traffic and content are commercially strong.

In practical terms, merchant choice becomes part of conversion rate optimisation itself. The affiliate site does not stop influencing revenue when the user clicks. The quality of the destination merchant remains part of the monetisation system all the way through to approved commission.
