## Introduction

Affiliate disclosure placement affects more than legal compliance. On commercial [review pages]({{ 'making-money-from-creating-websites-containing-aff/making-money-from-cr-f649af-affiliate-review-pag-f66da7/' | relative_url }}), it changes how readers interpret recommendations before they click a button or affiliate link. A disclosure that appears only in a footer, sidebar, or separate legal page can make the page feel evasive. A disclosure that appears close to the recommendation, in plain language, usually increases perceived transparency and reduces the “hidden commission” problem that damages trust after the click.

For affiliate websites built around product reviews, comparisons, and “best” lists, disclosure placement is therefore part of conversion design. It helps readers understand the commercial relationship early enough to evaluate the recommendation properly, while also reducing regulatory risk under UK ASA/CAP rules and US FTC endorsement guidance. Regulators repeatedly emphasise that disclosures should be “clear and conspicuous”, easy to understand, and close to the endorsement or affiliate link itself. ## Why distance from the link matters

The biggest disclosure mistake on [affiliate review]({{ 'making-money-from-creating-websites-containing-aff/making-money-from-cr-f649af-affiliate-review-pag-f66da7/' | relative_url }}) pages is not the [wording]({{ 'making-money-from-creating-websites-containing-aff/making-money-from-cr-f649af-affiliate-disclosure-9d7a11-clear-affiliate-word-ac25e8/' | relative_url }}). It is placement.

Many affiliate sites technically disclose their commercial relationship somewhere on the page, but place it so far from the recommendation that readers encounter the affiliate button first and the disclosure later. Regulators increasingly treat this as inadequate because readers may make a decision before seeing the commercial context. The FTC repeatedly stresses that disclosures must be difficult to miss and close enough to the endorsement that users actually notice them during decision-making. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://federal-lawyer.com/ftc-defense/affiliate-disclosure/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: federal-lawyer.com">[Federal Lawyer]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">federal-lawyer.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Federal Lawyer Understanding the FTC&#x27;s Affiliate Disclosure Rules4</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Affiliate Disclosures Must Be “Clear and Conspicuous”... That structure asks the user to click before understanding the commercial relationship. Even if technically disclosed elsewhere, it weakens trust because the page appears to prioritise conversion over clarity.

From a monetisation perspective, this can hurt long-term revenue quality. Readers who feel misled after discovering affiliate commissions may avoid future clicks, distrust reviews, or leave the site permanently. Short-term click-through gains can therefore reduce repeat visitor value and brand trust over time.

For scalable website systems, this creates a useful implementation rule:

* disclosures should appear before or immediately adjacent to monetised recommendations;
* disclosure placement should be template-driven rather than manually added;
* high-intent conversion modules should automatically include disclosure text nearby.

That matters especially for large review networks where pages are generated programmatically. Consistent placement reduces compliance drift across thousands of pages.

## Plain wording readers understand

Disclosure wording often fails because affiliate sites write for lawyers instead of readers.

Readers do not need a dense legal paragraph explaining commercial arrangements. They need a fast, plain-English explanation before the click.

Research into affiliate disclosure comprehension found that vague or abbreviated disclosures are frequently misunderstood by users, while explanatory disclosures improve recognition of advertising relationships. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1809.00620" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">arXivEndorsements on Social Media: An Empirical Study of Affiliate Marketing Disclosures on YouTube and PinterestSeptember 3, 2018...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 3, 2018</span></span></span>

Weak wording includes phrases such as:

* “Affiliate links may be present”;
* “Monetised links”;
* “Commercial arrangements may apply”;
* “Supported by partners”;
* “We participate in programmes”.

These phrases are technically suggestive but not immediately clear.

Stronger wording explains the reader outcome directly:

* “We may earn a commission if you buy through links on this page.”
* “This review contains affiliate links. We may receive payment if you purchase after clicking.”
* “If you buy through our links, we may earn a commission at no extra cost to you.”

The key is instant comprehension.

The ASA and CMA guidance around influencer and affiliate disclosures repeatedly emphasises that disclosures should be immediately recognisable and understandable to ordinary users, not hidden behind jargon or vague labels. For product review pages specifically, the wording should match the page style. A consumer advice review page can use conversational disclosure language without sounding defensive or overly corporate.

For example:

> “We test products independently. Some links on this page are affiliate links, which means we may earn a commission if you buy.”

This works because it combines:

* editorial positioning;
* commercial transparency;
* and reader-focused clarity.

Importantly, good disclosure wording does not need to reduce persuasion. On many review sites, transparent disclosure actually reinforces credibility because the reader sees that the commercial relationship is acknowledged openly rather than concealed.

## Top-of-page disclosure versus button-level disclosure

Different disclosure placements solve different problems. The strongest review pages often combine multiple disclosure layers instead of relying on a single site-wide disclaimer.

### Top-of-page disclosure

A short disclosure near the beginning of the article establishes commercial context early.

Typical placement includes:

* beneath the headline;
* below author information;
* before the first affiliate recommendation;
* or immediately after the introduction.

This works well because readers understand the business model before engaging deeply with the review.

Example:

> “This page contains affiliate links. If you purchase through them, we may earn a commission.”

For large affiliate sites, this is highly scalable because it can be embedded automatically into review templates, comparison templates, and buyer-guide [page types]({{ 'making-money-from-creating-websites-containing-aff/making-money-from-cr-f649af-buyer-intent-revenue-fe10e4-affiliate-page-types-956d68/' | relative_url }}).

However, top-of-page disclosure alone may not be enough on long pages with multiple conversion modules. Readers often scroll directly to buttons, [comparison tables]({{ 'making-money-from-creating-websites-containing-aff/making-money-from-cr-f649af-best-of-affiliate-pa-55feea-comparison-tables-cl-5eb30a/' | relative_url }}), or “best overall” sections without reading the introduction.

That is why many strong affiliate pages also use local disclosures near high-intent click areas.

* Google jump links;
* mobile scrolling;

* disclosures pushed below sticky buttons;
* font sizes too small to notice;
* affiliate labels hidden behind expandable sections;
* CTA buttons appearing before disclosure text due to responsive reordering.

For affiliate review systems operating at scale, mobile-first disclosure testing matters because many affiliate clicks occur on phones.

Practical implementation rules include:

* keep disclosure text directly above or below the button in mobile layouts;
* avoid light grey disclosure text on white backgrounds;
* do not place disclosures inside tabs or accordions;
* ensure disclosures remain visible before the affiliate click occurs.

This is partly compliance, but also usability. Readers scanning quickly on mobile are more sensitive to perceived manipulation or hidden intent.

## Disclosure as a trust signal rather than a legal interruption

Some affiliate publishers still treat disclosures as conversion obstacles. That often leads to minimised text, low-contrast styling, or attempts to make disclosures visually invisible while remaining technically present.

In practice, transparent disclosure frequently improves trust on high-intent review pages because it removes uncertainty about how the site makes money.

Readers already understand that many review sites earn commissions. The trust damage usually comes from concealment, not from the commission itself.

This changes how disclosure should be framed within a monetisation system. Disclosure is not merely:

* legal overhead;
* compliance boilerplate;
* or a mandatory interruption.

It is part of the page’s credibility architecture.

A trustworthy review page signals:

* how products were chosen;
* how the site earns money;
* where affiliate relationships exist; <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.rpclegal.com/snapshots/advertising-and-marketing/spring-2023/the-asas-strict-approach-to-affiliate-marketing-links-and-the-need-for-advertising-disclosures/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rpclegal.com">[rpclegal.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rpclegal.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Both affiliate marketers and the...Read more...</span></span></span>
* and whether recommendations are influenced commercially.

That clarity can improve reader confidence before the click.

For scalable affiliate publishing systems, disclosure placement therefore becomes a reusable trust pattern. High-performing review templates often standardise:

* disclosure position;
* wording style;
* font treatment;
* spacing around CTA buttons;
* and disclosure behaviour across devices.

That consistency helps readers recognise the site as transparent and predictable, which can support higher long-term revenue per visitor than aggressive but opaque conversion tactics.
