## Introduction

Affiliate comparison pages work because they meet readers at a late stage of the buying journey: the visitor is no longer asking “what is this category?” but “which of these named products should I choose?” That makes the page commercially valuable, but also more demanding. A good comparison page does not simply place two product names in a title and add affiliate buttons. It narrows the decision, explains the real trade-offs, shows which product fits which buyer, and sends the reader to a suitable merchant only after the recommendation feels earned.

For affiliate websites, these pages sit between broad “best of” guides and individual product reviews. They are most useful when a reader has already shortlisted two or three options, such as “Dyson V15 vs Shark Stratos”, “NordVPN vs Surfshark”, or “iPhone 15 vs Pixel 8”. Google’s review guidance explicitly recognises head-to-head comparisons as review content and says high-quality reviews should explain comparable alternatives, key decision factors, benefits, drawbacks, and what sets products apart. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://developers.google.com/search/docs/specialty/ecommerce/write-high-quality-reviews" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: developers.google.com">[Google for Developers]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">developers.google.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Google for DevelopersHow To Write Reviews | Google Search Central | Documentation | Google for DevelopersDecember 10, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 10, 2025</span></span></span>

## Why named-product comparisons are high-value affiliate pages

A visitor who searches for two named products is usually past casual browsing. They know the category, recognise the brands, and are trying to resolve a specific choice. That is why comparison pages can have a stronger conversion path than broad informational pages: the page is not creating demand from scratch; it is helping the reader choose where to place demand that already exists.

The commercial logic is simple. A broad guide such as “best cordless vacuum cleaners” may attract many visitors, but those visitors may still be deciding budget, features, brand, and whether they need the product at all. A page such as “Dyson V15 vs Shark Stratos” captures a narrower but more decisive moment. The reader has already reduced the market to named options. The affiliate page earns its commission by helping them avoid the wrong purchase, not by overwhelming them with every possible alternative.

This is also why comparison pages are useful in a scalable affiliate system. They can be generated from repeatable patterns: product A versus product B, product A versus cheaper alternative, current model versus previous model, brand leader versus specialist competitor, or premium product versus value product. The same underlying page type can serve electronics, software, appliances, garden tools, mattresses, insurance products, and many other commercial categories, provided the comparison [criteria]({{ 'making-money-from-creating-websites-containing-aff/making-money-from-cr-f649af-affiliate-comparison-5755f1-category-specific-cr-a92f21/' | relative_url }}) are specific to the category rather than generic.

The model has been commercially proven by product recommendation publishers. The Wirecutter, founded as a product recommendation site and later bought by The New York Times for more than $30 million, built its business around rigorously researched service journalism funded partly by affiliate links. Contemporary reports on the acquisition described affiliate commissions from purchases through e-commerce links as central to the revenue model. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://techcrunch.com/2016/10/24/nyt-acquires-wirecutter/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: techcrunch.com">[TechCrunch]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">techcrunch.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">TechCrunchThe New York Times acquires gadget recommendation site The Wirecutter | TechCrunchOctober 24, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 24, 2016</span></span></span> <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.cnbc.com/2016/10/24/the-new-york-times-is-buying-the-wirecutter-for-more-than-30-million.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cnbc.com">[CNBC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cnbc.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The New York Times is buying The Wirecutter for more than $30 million</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The New York Times is buying The Wirecutter for more than $30 million</span></span></span>

## Head-to-head product structures

A comparison page should make the choice visible early. The reader has not arrived to admire a long introduction; they want to know whether product A or product B is more suitable. The best structure gives a quick answer first, then supports it with [evidence]({{ 'making-money-from-creating-websites-containing-aff/making-money-from-cr-f649af-best-of-affiliate-pa-55feea-evidence-blocks-affi-b0d439/' | relative_url }}), trade-offs, and buying paths.

A practical head-to-head structure usually includes:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. **A direct verdict near the top.** State which product is better overall, which is better for a specific user, and when the other product still makes sense.
2. **A short comparison table.** Compare the few attributes that genuinely change the buying decision, such as price, warranty, battery life, suction power, platform support, contract length, replacement parts, or subscription cost.
3. **A decision-focused explanation.** Explain why those differences matter in real use, not just what the specification sheet says.
4. **Choose-this-if blocks.** Give each product a clear buyer profile.
5. **Merchant options or affiliate buttons.** Place links where the reader has enough confidence to act, not before the page has answered the comparison.

</div>

This page type is different from a “best products” list. A list can rank ten options because the reader is still exploring. A head-to-head page should be tighter. It should reduce uncertainty between named choices. Adding too many unrelated alternatives can weaken the page because it reopens the decision the reader came to close.

Baymard Institute’s e-commerce UX research helps explain why side-by-side structure matters. In spec-driven categories, users often struggle to compare products from separate product pages because they must remember details while switching between pages or scrolling. Baymard reports that 67% of participants in its testing on spec-driven sites used comparison features, while 17% of benchmarked spec-driven sites failed to offer them. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://baymard.com/blog/provide-comparison-features" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: baymard.com">[Baymard Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">baymard.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Source details in endnotes.</span></span></span>

For affiliate content, that finding translates into a clear page design rule: do the comparison work on the visitor’s behalf. Do not force readers to open several merchant tabs and mentally compare specifications. The affiliate page should become the decision layer between search and [merchant checkout]({{ 'making-money-from-creating-websites-containing-aff/making-money-from-cr-f649af-affiliate-offer-sele-904ed4-merchant-checkout-tr-71ffde/' | relative_url }}).

## Decision-focused comparison criteria

The most common failure of affiliate comparison pages is comparing what is easy to scrape rather than what matters to the buyer. A table filled with dimensions, colours, launch dates, and copied manufacturer claims may look complete while doing little to help the decision.

A stronger comparison starts with the purchase question behind the query. For example:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--checklist" markdown="1">

* For laptops, the decisive criteria may be processor generation, RAM, screen quality, battery life, repairability, keyboard comfort, ports, weight, and price.
* For cordless vacuums, they may be cleaning performance on carpets and hard floors, bin size, battery runtime, weight, filtration, noise, tools, and replacement battery cost.
* For web hosting, they may be renewal pricing, support quality, uptime evidence, storage limits, migration help, speed, contract length, and refund policy.
* For mattresses, they may be firmness, sleep position fit, trial period, returns process, edge support, motion isolation, heat retention, and delivery conditions.
* For software, they may be workflow fit, integrations, seat pricing, data export, support, learning curve, and lock-in risk.

</div>

Google’s review guidance is directly relevant here. It recommends quantitative measurements where possible, benefits and drawbacks based on original research, evidence of experience, explanation of what sets a product apart, and discussion of which option is best for particular uses or circumstances. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://developers.google.com/search/docs/specialty/ecommerce/write-high-quality-reviews" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: developers.google.com">[Google for Developers]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">developers.google.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Google for DevelopersHow To Write Reviews | Google Search Central | Documentation | Google for DevelopersDecember 10, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 10, 2025</span></span></span>

The useful distinction is between **feature comparison** and **decision comparison**. Feature comparison says: “Product A has 60 minutes of battery life and Product B has 45 minutes.” Decision comparison says: “Choose Product A if you clean a whole house in one session; choose Product B if you live in a flat and care more about lower weight and price.” The second version turns facts into buying guidance.

For a scalable affiliate system, this means comparison templates should not use the same generic table across every niche. They need category-specific decision fields. A vacuum comparison should not feel like a credit-card comparison with different labels. The repeatable part is the structure; the criteria need to match the product category.

## Choose-this-if recommendation blocks

“Choose this if” blocks are especially useful on affiliate comparison pages because they allow more than one product to be legitimately recommended. A head-to-head page does not always need one absolute winner. Often, the honest answer is that one product is better for budget buyers, another for heavy users, and another for people already locked into a particular ecosystem.

A good choose-this-if block is short, specific, and buyer-led:

**Choose Product A if** you want the strongest performance, can justify the higher price, and will use the advanced features often enough for them to matter.

**Choose Product B if** you want most of the core benefit for less money, prefer simpler controls, or are buying for occasional use rather than daily use.

**Avoid Product A if** the higher running cost, subscription fee, size, weight, or replacement-part price would become frustrating after purchase.

**Avoid Product B if** you need the premium feature that actually separates the two products, not just the brand name.

These blocks do two commercial jobs at once. They improve reader trust because the page is not pretending every product is perfect, and they increase click quality because visitors self-select into the offer that fits them. Click quality matters because [affiliate revenue]({{ 'making-money-from-creating-websites-containing-aff/making-money-from-cr-f649af-affiliate-conversion-fac650-affiliate-revenue-le-8c2b2f/' | relative_url }}) depends not only on click-through rate but also on merchant conversion, average order value, commission rate, and approval rate.

Choose-this-if blocks also help prevent a common affiliate mistake: recommending the highest-commission product to everyone. That may raise short-term clicks, but it can lower trust, increase returns, and weaken the site’s long-term performance. Regulators and search platforms both create pressure against misleading or thin recommendations. The FTC says affiliate relationships should be disclosed clearly and conspicuously, close to the recommendation where possible, so readers can judge how much weight to give the endorsement. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://consumer.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/ftcs-endorsement-guides-what-people-are-asking" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: consumer.ftc.gov">[Consumer Advice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">consumer.ftc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Source details in endnotes.</span></span></span>

## The comparison table should reduce effort, not display everything

[Comparison tables]({{ 'making-money-from-creating-websites-containing-aff/making-money-from-cr-f649af-best-of-affiliate-pa-55feea-comparison-tables-cl-5eb30a/' | relative_url }}) are often treated as a design feature, but they are really a decision tool. Their job is not to prove that the page contains data. Their job is to reduce the reader’s effort at the exact point where two products are difficult to separate.

A useful affiliate comparison table has three qualities.

First, it compares the same attributes across every product. If one column has “fast charging”, another has “premium design”, and another has “great reviews”, the reader cannot make a fair comparison. The rows should be consistent.

Second, it separates factual attributes from judgement. “Price”, “weight”, “trial period”, and “battery life” are factual fields. “Best for families” or “better for beginners” are editorial judgements. Both can be useful, but they should not be mixed carelessly.

Third, it avoids false precision. If the site has not tested two products, it should not imply lab certainty. A page can still be useful by citing manufacturer specifications, public policies, merchant terms, credible third-party tests, or clearly marked user-review patterns, but it should be honest about the basis for the comparison.

Baymard’s research on comparison tools shows that comparison features can help users assess many specifications [side by side]({{ 'making-money-from-creating-websites-containing-aff/making-money-from-cr-f649af-affiliate-comparison-5755f1-side-by-side-compari-7c4125/' | relative_url }}), especially in spec-heavy categories, but also notes that these tools can be hard to use if selection and comparison are poorly implemented. Persistent product selection controls and reminders of chosen products were among the implementation details Baymard found useful in testing. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://baymard.com/blog/provide-comparison-features" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: baymard.com">[Baymard Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">baymard.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Source details in endnotes.</span></span></span>

For affiliate pages, the equivalent is editorial discipline. The table should not become a dumping ground for every available attribute. It should highlight the decision points that make a reader think, “That settles it.”

## Historical pattern: from editorial reviews to affiliate decision pages

Product comparison pages are part of a wider shift in publishing. Traditional product journalism, consumer testing, SEO content, and affiliate monetisation have converged into a common format: pages that help readers choose products and then earn revenue from the resulting purchase.

The Wirecutter example is important because it showed that affiliate-funded product guidance could be positioned as service journalism rather than merely as advertising. Reports around its New York Times acquisition emphasised both the attractiveness of the affiliate revenue model and the importance of rigorous research and editorial integrity. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://money.cnn.com/2016/10/24/media/the-new-york-times-buys-wirecutter/index.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: money.cnn.com">[money.cnn.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">money.cnn.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">New York Times buying The Wirecutter, and a new revenue stream</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">New York Times buying The Wirecutter, and a new revenue stream</span></span></span>

That history also explains a tension. Readers value comparison pages because they save time, but they may distrust them if the commercial incentive is hidden or if the recommendations feel like commission-driven listicles. This tension is especially visible in product review search results, where many pages use similar titles, similar [tables]({{ 'making-money-from-creating-websites-containing-aff/making-money-from-cr-f649af-best-of-affiliate-pa-55feea-comparison-tables-cl-5eb30a/' | relative_url }}), and similar affiliate buttons.

Google’s documentation reflects that tension. Its reviews system aims to reward review content that provides insightful analysis and original research rather than thin summaries, and it applies to standalone content that gives recommendations, opinions, or analysis, including head-to-head comparisons. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://developers.google.com/search/docs/specialty/ecommerce/write-high-quality-reviews" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: developers.google.com">[Google for Developers]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">developers.google.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Google for DevelopersHow To Write Reviews | Google Search Central | Documentation | Google for DevelopersDecember 10, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 10, 2025</span></span></span>

## Where comparison pages fit in an affiliate site architecture

Comparison pages are strongest when they are part of a cluster, not isolated one-offs. A mature affiliate site may have a broad category guide, individual product reviews, brand comparison pages, and head-to-head pages that serve different stages of the buying journey.

A useful structure might look like this:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* **Category guide:** “Best cordless vacuum cleaners”
* **Product review:** “Dyson V15 Detect review”
* **Alternative page:** “Best Dyson alternatives”
* **Head-to-head page:** “Dyson V15 vs Shark Stratos”
* **Use-case page:** “Best cordless vacuum for pet hair”
* **Commercial support page:** “Where to buy Dyson V15 in the UK”

</div>

The comparison page can receive internal links from the broad guide, the individual reviews, and the alternatives page. It can also link back to those pages when the reader needs more depth. This creates a user flow that mirrors real buying behaviour: discover the category, shortlist products, compare named options, then click through to a merchant.

For a website-generation system, comparison pages are useful because they can be identified from entity pairs. Products, brands, models, subscription plans, and product generations can all become comparison entities. However, not every possible pair deserves a page. The best candidates usually have:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--benefit" markdown="1">

* both products known enough for people to search them by name;
* meaningful overlap in use case;
* real differences that affect buying decisions;
* affiliate offers available for at least one product, ideally both;
* enough trustworthy information to support a fair comparison;
* commercial intent visible in search behaviour.

</div>

A “versus” page for two products that are rarely considered together may attract little traffic and confuse readers. A comparison between near-identical variants may be better handled inside one product review or buying guide. The goal is not to create every possible pair; it is to create the pairs that match real decision friction.

## Offer selection on comparison pages

Comparison pages create a special monetisation challenge: the honest recommendation may not always match the best commission. A page that compares two named products should ideally give the reader a path to buy either product, especially if both are legitimate choices for different users.

Google’s review guidance says publishers should consider linking to multiple sellers so readers can purchase from their merchant of choice. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://developers.google.com/search/docs/specialty/ecommerce/write-high-quality-reviews" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: developers.google.com">[Google for Developers]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">developers.google.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Google for DevelopersHow To Write Reviews | Google Search Central | Documentation | Google for DevelopersDecember 10, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 10, 2025</span></span></span> That matters for affiliate conversion as well as reader trust. If only one merchant is shown, the page can feel biased or inconvenient. If several reputable merchants are shown, the reader has more ways to act.

Offer matching should consider:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--decision" markdown="1">

* **Stock availability:** a top recommendation with poor availability may waste clicks.
* **Merchant trust:** a recognised retailer may convert better than an obscure merchant with a slightly higher commission.
* **Total cost:** shipping, subscriptions, installation, accessories, and renewal prices may matter more than headline price.
* **Regional relevance:** UK visitors need UK pricing, delivery, warranty, and consumer terms where possible.
* **Device and urgency:** mobile users may prefer a fast merchant checkout; high-consideration purchases may need more supporting information.
* **Commission reliability:** high advertised rates are less useful if tracking, approval, or merchant conversion is weak.

</div>

A comparison page can also monetise different outcomes. If Product A is best overall but Product B is better value, both can receive affiliate links in context. If neither product is ideal for a certain buyer, the page can link internally to a better alternatives guide. That internal link may be more valuable than forcing an unsuitable affiliate click.

## Disclosure and trust on affiliate comparison pages

Affiliate comparison pages need clear disclosure because the commercial relationship is directly tied to the recommendation. In the UK, CAP guidance describes affiliate marketers as secondary advertisers when they earn money in direct proportion to the clicks or sales they generate, and says affiliate marketing must be obviously identifiable as advertising. For blogs and news-style sites, CAP warns that disclosures at the bottom of a post are unlikely to be sufficient where readers encounter affiliate-linked claims earlier. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/affiliate-marketing.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: asa.org.uk">[ASA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">asa.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ASAOnline Affiliate Marketing</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ASAOnline Affiliate Marketing</span></span></span>

The same principle appears in US FTC guidance. The FTC says affiliate marketers should clearly and conspicuously disclose their relationship to retailers, and that the closer the disclosure is to the recommendation, the better. It also notes that terms such as “affiliate link” may not be clear to all consumers. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://consumer.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/ftcs-endorsement-guides-what-people-are-asking" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: consumer.ftc.gov">[Consumer Advice]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">consumer.ftc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Source details in endnotes.</span></span></span>

For comparison pages, disclosure should be built into the template rather than handled manually after publication. A clear line near the top can explain that the page may earn commission from links and that this does not change the reader’s price. Where individual links are affiliate links, they can also be marked in context. The wording should be plain, not evasive.

Disclosure is not only a compliance issue. It is a conversion issue. A reader choosing between two named products is already alert to bias. If the page hides its commercial interest, the recommendation becomes less credible. If the page is upfront and still gives balanced advice, the affiliate link can feel like a convenient next step rather than a trick.

## Common failure modes

Comparison pages often fail in predictable ways. These failures are especially important for high-volume affiliate sites because the same weakness can be repeated across hundreds of pages.

**The fake comparison.** The page uses a “Product A vs Product B” title but gives a generic summary of both products without a real verdict. The reader still has to decide alone.

**The copied specification table.** The table contains manufacturer data but no explanation of which differences matter. This is thin content disguised as completeness.

**The commission-biased winner.** The recommended product is always the one with the better affiliate programme. Readers notice patterns like this, especially in categories where enthusiasts know the market.

**The missing negative case.** Every product is described positively, and no one is told who should avoid each option. This makes the page feel promotional rather than useful.

**The outdated comparison.** Prices, model versions, warranties, stock, and software features change. A comparison between named products can become misleading faster than an evergreen explainer.

**The wrong intent match.** Some “versus” searches are not purchase searches. They may be troubleshooting, compatibility, fandom, legal, or technical queries. Affiliate links only work when the intent supports a buying path.

**The mobile table problem.** Wide comparison tables can be awkward on phones. If the reader cannot scan the decision points quickly, the page loses one of its main advantages. Baymard’s mobile testing found comparison features less useful on mobile partly because small viewports limit side-by-side viewing. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://baymard.com/blog/provide-comparison-features" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: baymard.com">[Baymard Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">baymard.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Source details in endnotes.</span></span></span>

Avoiding these failures requires more than better copy. It requires page templates that force meaningful verdicts, category-specific criteria, update checks, disclosure placement, and mobile-friendly comparison layouts.

## How to decide whether a comparison page is worth creating

A scalable affiliate system should evaluate comparison opportunities before creating pages. The question is not “can these two entities be compared?” but “does this comparison help a buyer make a commercial decision?”

A practical decision rule is:

**Create a comparison page when two named products are close substitutes, there is evidence of decision friction, the differences can be explained clearly, and the page can route the reader to relevant offers or deeper internal pages.**

Do not create the page if the products are unrelated, if the answer is obvious, if one product is discontinued with no buying path, or if there is not enough reliable information to compare them fairly.

The best candidates often come from recurring patterns:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* **Market leader vs challenger:** “Dyson vs Shark”, “Shopify vs Wix”, “Ring vs Eufy”.
* **Current model vs older model:** useful when buyers are deciding whether the new version is worth the extra cost.
* **Premium vs budget:** useful when the reader wants to know what they lose by spending less.
* **Two products from the same brand:** useful when variant names are confusing.
* **Two subscription tiers:** useful for software, memberships, warranties, and services.
* **Product vs alternative:** useful when one product is famous but not always the best fit.

</div>

This approach keeps the site aligned with revenue per visitor. A page with modest traffic can still be valuable if the visitor is close to purchase and the affiliate path is clear. Conversely, a high-volume comparison with weak commercial intent may be less profitable than it appears.

## What a strong affiliate comparison page actually does

A strong comparison page performs a specific job: it turns a named-product dilemma into a confident next action. It does not need to cover the entire category, review every competitor, or repeat a broad buying guide. It needs to answer the reader’s immediate question better than the reader could answer it by opening two merchant tabs.

The page should make the trade-off visible: performance versus price, features versus simplicity, premium build versus cheaper replacement, ecosystem lock-in versus flexibility, short-term discount versus long-term cost. It should also explain where the recommendation changes depending on the buyer. That is where comparison pages become more commercially useful than simple rankings.

For affiliate monetisation, the most valuable comparison pages are not the ones with the most links. They are the ones where the reader reaches the merchant already knowing why they are clicking. That produces a cleaner conversion path: matched intent, reduced uncertainty, relevant offer, clear disclosure, and a purchase decision that feels like the reader’s own.
