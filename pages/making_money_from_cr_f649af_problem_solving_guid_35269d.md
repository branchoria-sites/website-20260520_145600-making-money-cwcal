## Introduction

Problem-solving affiliate guides are pages that begin with a reader’s situation before they introduce products. Instead of starting with “best cordless drills” or “top running shoes”, they start with the problem behind the purchase: “how to drill into brick without cracking it”, “why running shoes hurt the outside of my foot”, or “how to stop condensation in a small flat”. Their job is to diagnose the need, explain the decision, and then route the reader into the right product category at the moment a product becomes useful.


<img src="{{ "/assets/images/making_money_from_cr_f649af_problem_solving_guid_35269d-overview.png" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Problem Guides" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">
This matters because many profitable affiliate journeys do not begin with a product name. They begin with uncertainty. A reader may know the symptom, task, constraint, or frustration, but not the product category that solves it. A strong problem guide turns that uncertainty into a clearer buying path. It can send one reader to dehumidifiers, another to extractor fans, and another to window insulation film, without pretending that every problem has one universal “best” product.

For websites that make money from affiliate links, this page type is valuable because it sits between pure information and [direct comparison]({{ 'untitled/verdicts-50c825/' | relative_url }}). It can attract earlier-stage searches, build trust through useful diagnosis, and then pass qualified visitors into product lists, [comparison pages]({{ 'untitled/comparison-pages/' | relative_url }}), reviews, or merchant pages. Done well, it is not a thin affiliate page; it adds value by helping the reader understand what kind of product they need and why.

## Why problem guides convert differently from product pages

A direct product page serves readers who already know the category. A problem-solving guide serves readers who know the outcome they want but have not yet chosen the route. That distinction changes the page structure, the link placement, and the type of affiliate offer that belongs on the page.

Search and ecommerce research both show why this matters. Product-related searches often contain intent signals that are not just product names: people search by attributes, use cases, constraints, and symptoms. Research on ecommerce query intent notes that search engines can struggle when they fail to identify which terms in a query express the user’s product intent, especially where there is a vocabulary gap between the reader’s words and the product catalogue’s [wording]({{ 'untitled/wording/' | relative_url }}). In affiliate content, problem guides can close that gap before the reader reaches a product grid. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.08564" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">arXiv Intent term selection and refinement in e-commerce queries</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">arXivIntent term selection and refinement in e-commerce queriesAugust 22, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 22, 2019</span></span></span>

A useful example is “best heater for damp room”. The reader may think they need a heater, but the underlying problem might be moisture, poor ventilation, or cold surfaces. A weak affiliate page would immediately list electric heaters. A stronger problem guide would explain when heat helps, when it does not, and when the relevant product category is instead a dehumidifier, extractor fan, draught-proofing kit, or mould treatment. That diagnosis may reduce the number of irrelevant clicks, but the clicks it does earn are more commercially qualified.

This also explains why problem guides should not be treated as ordinary blog posts with affiliate links added at the end. Their commercial function is routing. They move the reader from “what is going wrong?” to “which class of solution should I compare?” The affiliate link is not the whole conversion event; it is one step in a guided path.

## Diagnosis-first content structures

The strongest problem-solving affiliate guides usually follow a diagnosis-first pattern. They identify the problem, separate common causes, show decision [criteria]({{ 'untitled/criteria/' | relative_url }}), and only then introduce product categories. This protects the page from becoming a disguised product list and helps readers understand why one product type fits their situation better than another.

A practical structure is:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. **Name the problem in the reader’s language.** Use the wording a reader would naturally use: “laptop overheating on a desk”, “dog pulling on lead”, “garden furniture blowing over”, “weak Wi-Fi in back bedroom”.
2. **Separate likely causes or situations.** Do not assume the same product solves every version of the problem.
3. **Match each cause to a product category.** Route to categories, not just individual products.
4. **Explain when not to buy.** This builds trust and prevents poor-fit clicks.
5. **Link onwards to the next page type.** Send readers to buying guides, comparisons, reviews, category pages, or merchant pages depending on readiness.

</div>

The diagnosis stage is where the affiliate page creates original value. Google’s guidance on helpful content asks whether a page provides original information, analysis, or substantial added value beyond obvious summaries, and its spam policies warn against thin affiliate pages that copy merchant descriptions without meaningful extra value. A problem guide earns its place by adding the missing decision logic between the problem and the product. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: developers.google.com">[Google for Developers]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">developers.google.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Google for DevelopersCreating Helpful, Reliable, People-First Content | Google Search Central | Documentation | Google for DevelopersDece...</span></span></span>

For example, a page on “how to make a rented flat warmer” should not simply list heaters. It can split the issue into draughts, window heat loss, poor insulation, radiator inefficiency, and personal warmth. Each branch points towards different product categories: draught excluders, thermal curtains, radiator reflector panels, heated throws, smart thermostats, or portable heaters. That is a richer and more useful commercial journey than a generic “best heaters” article.

The diagnosis can be presented as prose, a decision tree, a comparison block, or a short checklist. The format matters less than the logic: the reader should leave the first half of the page with a clearer understanding of what kind of solution fits their situation.

## When to introduce product links

Product links should appear when the reader has enough context to understand why the product category is relevant. Introduce them too early and the guide feels like an advert. Introduce them too late and commercially ready readers may leave before reaching the next step.

A useful rule is to link at category-confirmation moments. These are points where the page has just helped the reader identify a likely solution path. For instance:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--example" markdown="1">

* After explaining that condensation caused by daily drying of clothes indoors is often a moisture-removal problem, link to a dehumidifier guide.
* After explaining that heel pain during walking may relate to support, cushioning, or fit, link to walking shoe and insole guides rather than one generic shoe offer.
* After explaining that poor Wi-Fi in one room can come from distance, wall thickness, router placement, or dead zones, link to mesh Wi-Fi systems, range extenders, or powerline adapters according to the scenario.

</div>

This approach also aligns with [disclosure]({{ 'untitled/disclosure/' | relative_url }}) and trust requirements. In the UK, the Advertising Standards Authority says affiliate marketing must be obviously identifiable, and that affiliates are effectively acting as secondary advertisers when they earn in direct proportion to the interest they generate. The FTC similarly says affiliate relationships should be disclosed clearly and conspicuously, close enough to the recommendation that readers understand the connection. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/affiliate-marketing.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: asa.org.uk">[ASA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">asa.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ASAOnline Affiliate Marketing</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ASA | CAPMarch 22, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 22, 2023</span></span></span>

For problem guides, that means the disclosure should not be hidden below a long article or separated from the first commercial recommendation. A plain statement near the top and clear labelling around affiliate sections is usually more reader-friendly than vague wording such as “affiliate link” alone, which the FTC says consumers may not fully understand. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/ftcs-endorsement-guides" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ftc.gov">[Federal Trade Commission]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ftc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Source details in endnotes.</span></span></span>

The link itself should also match the reader’s stage. A reader who has just learned that there are three possible solution categories may need a comparison of categories, not an immediate merchant link. A reader who has already reached a clear category choice may be ready for “best dehumidifiers for small flats” or a product comparison. The more uncertain the problem, the more the page should route internally before sending the visitor off-site.

## Internal linking into money pages

Problem guides are especially useful in an affiliate site because they can feed more commercial pages without forcing every search query into a “best product” template. They create a middle layer between informational demand and product-category revenue.

The [internal linking]({{ 'untitled/internal-linking/' | relative_url }}) model should be deliberate:

**Problem guide to category explainer.**

Use this when the reader still needs to understand the category. Example: “Why your room smells damp” links to “dehumidifiers versus air purifiers”.

**Problem guide to buying guide.**

Use this when the reader knows the category but needs selection help. Example: “How to stop a dog pulling” links to “best no-pull dog harnesses”.

**Problem guide to comparison page.**

Use this when the main uncertainty is between two solution types. Example: “mesh Wi-Fi or powerline adapter for thick walls”.

**Problem guide to product review.**

Use this when the page identifies a specific use case that one product strongly addresses. Example: “best quiet dehumidifier for a bedroom” can link into a review if the review is genuinely relevant.

**Problem guide to merchant or offer page.**

Use this only when the reader is clearly ready to act, such as after a checklist confirms the product type and the offer is tightly matched.

This is where ecommerce navigation research is useful. Baymard describes category pages as navigational hubs that help users orient themselves and choose the right subcategory, especially in large catalogues. It also notes that category pages can surface buying guides and help content where users need guidance before choosing. Affiliate problem guides can play a similar role: they do not replace money pages, but they make the route into them more precise. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://baymard.com/learn/ecommerce-category-page" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: baymard.com">[Baymard Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">baymard.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Institute Ecommerce Category Pages: Definition, Usage, &amp; Design Tips – Baymard</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Institute Ecommerce Category Pages: Definition, Usage, &amp; Design Tips – Baymard</span></span></span>

The same principle applies to information scent, the cues that help a user decide whether a link will take them closer to their goal. If links are vague, users make wrong turns or backtrack. Recent research modelling information scent describes how users often inspect pages only “just enough” before choosing a link, which makes clear labels and local context important. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.08564" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">arXiv Intent term selection and refinement in e-commerce queries</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">arXivIntent term selection and refinement in e-commerce queriesAugust 22, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 22, 2019</span></span></span>

For affiliate sites, this means internal links should be specific rather than generic. “See recommended products” is weaker than “Compare compact dehumidifiers for bedrooms”. “Shop now” is weaker than “See harnesses for dogs that pull”. The destination should feel like the next logical step in the diagnosis, not a sudden commercial detour.

## Mapping problems to product categories

A scalable affiliate site needs repeatable ways to turn problem topics into product-category routes. The goal is not to hand-craft every page from scratch, but to classify problem intent accurately enough that the page can choose the right structure and links.

A practical mapping framework uses four questions:

**What is the reader trying to fix?**

This identifies the problem: pain, inconvenience, risk, inefficiency, waste, discomfort, uncertainty, or missing capability.

**What are the likely causes?**

This prevents over-routing to one product. A “cold room” may involve insulation, heating, draughts, humidity, or radiator performance.

**Which product categories solve each cause?**

This creates the commercial bridge. Each cause should have one or more relevant categories, with notes on when each is suitable.

**What proof or criteria help the reader choose?**

This could be measurements, compatibility, room size, material, safety rating, dimensions, running cost, care requirements, or user scenario.

A website generation system can use this framework to standardise [page types]({{ 'untitled/page-types/' | relative_url }}). For example:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* **Symptom-led guide:** “Why does my washing smell musty?” Routes to washing machine cleaners, laundry sanitisers, drying racks, dehumidifiers, or washer maintenance guides.
* **Task-led guide:** “How to cut laminate flooring neatly.” Routes to jigsaws, fine-tooth blades, laminate cutters, measuring tools, and safety equipment.
* **Constraint-led guide:** “Best way to dry clothes in a flat without a tumble dryer.” Routes to heated airers, dehumidifiers, drying racks, moisture absorbers, and ventilation products.
* **Compatibility-led guide:** “What charger do I need for this laptop?” Routes to USB-C chargers, barrel chargers, wattage guides, and brand-specific charger pages.
* **Risk-led guide:** “How to make stairs safer for an older dog.” Routes to stair gates, non-slip treads, ramps, harnesses, and joint-support products where appropriate.

</div>

The key is that the page should not force every problem into one affiliate offer. It should narrow the field. That is commercially useful because readers who click after accurate routing are more likely to land on a relevant money page.

## Where problem guides add commercial value

Problem guides often earn less per visit than direct “best product” pages if judged only by immediate affiliate clicks. Their value is broader. They capture earlier intent, qualify readers, strengthen topical clusters, and feed internal paths into higher-converting pages.

They are particularly useful where:

**The buyer does not know the product vocabulary.**

A reader may search for “stop shoes rubbing back of heel” rather than “heel grips” or “moleskin padding”. The guide can introduce the category in plain language.

**Several product categories could solve the same problem.**

A page on “how to keep a conservatory cool” might route to blinds, fans, window film, ventilation, portable air conditioners, or insulation improvements.

**The wrong product is a common mistake.**

A guide can explain why an air purifier will not remove damp, why a Wi-Fi extender may not solve all dead-zone problems, or why a cheap drill bit may fail in masonry.

**The purchase depends on context.**

Room size, pet breed, flooring type, skin sensitivity, device compatibility, budget, tenancy rules, and maintenance tolerance can all change the best category.

**The money page needs warmer traffic.**

A direct comparison page may convert better when visitors arrive already understanding the problem and the selection criteria.

This is also where problem guides avoid the “thin affiliation” risk. Google’s spam policy says good affiliate sites can add value through meaningful content, navigation of products or categories, product comparisons, original reviews, and useful information beyond merchant copy. A diagnosis-first page that routes readers to categories is closer to this value-adding model than a page that merely republishes product descriptions. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: developers.google.com">[Google for Developers]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">developers.google.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Google for DevelopersCreating Helpful, Reliable, People-First Content | Google Search Central | Documentation | Google for DevelopersDece...</span></span></span>

## Common failure modes

The biggest mistake is pretending a problem guide is neutral while pushing one product category from the start. Readers notice when the diagnosis is fake. If every symptom leads to the same offer, the page stops feeling helpful and starts feeling like a funnel.

Other common failures include:

**Starting with products before explaining the problem.**

This works for high-intent product searches but feels premature for problem searches.

**Using vague category links.**

Links such as “recommended tools” or “top picks” do not give enough information scent. Use labels that describe the next decision.

<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://coloriy.com/affiliate-disclosure/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: coloriy.com">[**Overloading the page with affiliate boxes.**]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">coloriy.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Affiliate Disclosure</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Affiliate Disclosure</span></span></span>

Too many product cards can interrupt the diagnostic flow. A problem guide should usually link to product-category pages rather than trying to monetise every paragraph.

**Ignoring “do not buy” cases.**

A trustworthy guide says when a product is unnecessary, unsuitable, or a poor first step. This may reduce low-quality clicks but can increase trust and repeat use.

**Making pages too generic to scale safely.**

A template that says “identify the cause, compare options, choose the best product” without specific causes, criteria, and category routes adds little value. Scalable content still needs distinctive decision logic for each problem cluster.

**Burying disclosure.**

Affiliate disclosure should be clear before or near commercial recommendations. FTC guidance stresses that disclosures must be understandable, close to the relevant claim or link, and prominent enough for consumers to notice and process. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/ftcs-endorsement-guides" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ftc.gov">[Federal Trade Commission]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ftc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Source details in endnotes.</span></span></span>

A more subtle failure is creating too many near-identical problem pages. For example, separate pages for “how to stop damp in bedroom”, “how to stop damp in small bedroom”, and “how to stop damp in cold bedroom” may become repetitive unless each page has distinct causes, product routes, and advice. At scale, the better pattern is often one strong guide with sections for common scenarios, supported by internal links to genuinely distinct money pages.

## A repeatable template for problem guides

A useful problem guide template should feel natural to the reader but disciplined enough to scale. The following structure works across many affiliate niches:


<img src="{{ "/assets/images/making_money_from_cr_f649af_problem_solving_guid_35269d-Illustration-3-light.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Problem Guides illustration 3" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/making_money_from_cr_f649af_problem_solving_guid_35269d-Illustration-3-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/making_money_from_cr_f649af_problem_solving_guid_35269d-Illustration-3-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low">
### Start with the reader’s immediate problem

Open by confirming the symptom or task in plain English. Give a direct answer early, but avoid pretending there is one universal product answer. For example: “If your windows are wet each morning, the problem is usually excess moisture meeting cold glass. The right solution depends on whether the moisture comes from drying clothes, poor ventilation, cold surfaces, or leaks.”

### Explain the main causes

Use a short diagnostic section. This can be a table, decision tree, or scenario list. Each cause should be concrete enough to route to a product category.

Example:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Wet windows after drying clothes indoors: moisture removal and ventilation.
* Black mould around frames: condensation control plus cleaning and prevention.
* Damp patches on one wall: possible maintenance issue before buying indoor products.
* Whole room feels cold and damp: heating, insulation, ventilation, and humidity need separating.

</div>

### Match causes to product categories

This is the commercial centre of the page. It should explain product categories as solutions, not as random recommendations. Use phrases such as “look at”, “compare”, or “start with” rather than pushing every reader to buy immediately.

Example:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--checklist" markdown="1">

* Compare compact dehumidifiers if moisture is the main issue.
* Look at thermal curtains or window film if cold glass is the trigger.
* Consider extractor fans or ventilation aids if steam is not leaving the room.
* Use mould removers only as part of prevention, not as the whole solution.

</div>

### Link to the right money page

Each route should have a natural destination. A broad category comparison might lead to “dehumidifier buying guide”. A specific scenario might lead to “best quiet dehumidifiers for bedrooms”. A product-type dispute might lead to “dehumidifier versus air purifier”.

### Add trust builders

Trust comes from specificity. Mention sizing, compatibility, safety, running costs, limitations, maintenance, and when to seek professional help if the issue is outside the product’s role. These details make the affiliate route more credible and reduce poor-fit clicks.

## How to decide whether a problem guide is worth creating

Not every problem query deserves its own affiliate guide. A scalable site should prioritise problems that can be diagnosed, routed, and monetised without misleading the reader.

A strong candidate usually has five traits:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. **The problem is common enough to attract search demand.**
2. **The reader is likely to take action after learning the cause.**
3. **There are multiple product categories or product attributes to explain.**
4. **Affiliate offers exist for the relevant solution categories.**
5. **The site can add value before the product click.**

</div>

A weak candidate has the opposite pattern: low commercial action, no clear product route, or advice that would be unsafe or misleading if simplified. For example, some home, health, finance, or safety problems require stronger caveats and may be unsuitable for direct affiliate routing unless handled with care and appropriate expertise.

The best clusters often come from recurring problem-to-category patterns. A pet site might build clusters around pulling, chewing, shedding, anxiety, travel, feeding, and joint comfort. A home site might build clusters around damp, storage, heating, cleaning, small-space living, noise, and garden maintenance. A tech site might build clusters around slow Wi-Fi, charging, storage, overheating, compatibility, and home-office setup.

Once one problem pattern proves useful, it can be repeated across adjacent pages with different specifics. The scalable element is the structure; the non-negotiable element is the diagnosis.

## The role of evidence and testing

Problem guides should be judged by more than page views. Their purpose is to move readers into a better product path, so measurement should focus on routing quality.

Useful metrics include:

* Click-through rate from problem guide to relevant internal money pages.
* Affiliate click-through rate from those money pages.
* Scroll depth to diagnosis and category-matching sections.
* Search queries that bring readers to the guide.
* Exit rate before the first useful route.
* Revenue per visitor by problem cluster, not just by individual page.
* Assisted revenue where a reader first lands on a problem guide and later converts through a buying guide or review.

Testing should focus on the diagnosis and routing points. For example, does a decision table outperform a long explanation? Do readers click more when product-category links appear after each cause rather than in one block near the end? Does a “which solution fits?” section improve downstream conversion compared with a simple list of recommended products?

This is where affiliate content and ecommerce UX overlap. Baymard’s research on product lists and filtering emphasises that product finding becomes difficult when users lack the right tools to evaluate and narrow options. A problem guide performs a similar narrowing function before the reader reaches the product list. <span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://baymard.com/learn/ecommerce-category-page" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: baymard.com">[Baymard Institute]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">baymard.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Institute Ecommerce Category Pages: Definition, Usage, &amp; Design Tips – Baymard</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Institute Ecommerce Category Pages: Definition, Usage, &amp; Design Tips – Baymard</span></span></span>

The most useful test is not “which page gets the most clicks?” but “which page sends the best-matched clicks?” A high click-through rate into the wrong category can create poor merchant conversion, low trust, and weak long-term revenue. A slightly lower click-through rate into the right buying guide may earn more over time.

## Why this page type belongs in an affiliate system

Problem-solving affiliate guides are not just supporting content. They are routing assets. They capture readers before they know the product category, diagnose the commercial need, and move them towards pages that can monetise more directly.

Their value is highest when they are built as part of a wider topic cluster. A single guide on “how to stop condensation” can link into dehumidifiers, window insulation, extractor fans, mould removers, and heating advice. Each of those money pages can link back to the problem guide where readers need diagnosis. The result is a more coherent site architecture: problem pages explain why, category pages explain what type, comparison pages explain which option, and reviews explain which product.

For high-volume affiliate website production, the lesson is clear. Do not treat every monetisable query as a product roundup. Some of the best affiliate journeys begin with a reader who cannot yet name the product they need. A well-built problem guide gives that reader the missing map.
